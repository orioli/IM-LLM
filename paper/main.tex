\title{Is less more?\\[0.4em]
\large Narrow context is more efficient than full context\\
for generative ideation with product graphs}
\author{[Author names and affiliations]}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Studies of AI for innovation mostly compare outcomes: LLM ideas against human ones, people with AI against people without. Such benchmarks expire as models improve and get cheaper. We ask a more durable question: how should the problem be presented to the model, measured as outcome per token? A hand-built innovation matrix of a microwave oven (123 nodes, 246 typed edges) was given to one model in four forms: the full graph, the vertical slice for the target market need, a random slice of the same size, or no graph. The model applied the SUBSTITUTE operator to 13 market needs at four levels of thinking effort (580 calls, 2,892 ideas), and a second model judged the ideas blind. Because brainstorming is a search for the rare excellent idea, the main measure is yield: top ideas (the top 12\% of all ideas) per 100k tokens. The market-need slice at medium effort yields 8.8 top ideas per 100k tokens, about three times the full graph at any effort, and is more efficient than the full graph on 10 of 13 market needs. The full graph writes the best-rated idea sets but no more top ideas, and per token it is less efficient than no graph at all. A random slice is the least efficient graph form, so relevance, not size, drives the gain. More thinking effort raises quality but not yield, and in an exploratory follow-up no persona beat a plain innovation engineer. Once a problem is framed, showing the model the part of the product that bears on it is worth more than showing it everything.
\end{abstract}
