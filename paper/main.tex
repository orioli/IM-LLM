\begin{abstract}
In AI-assisted product ideation, describing the problem as a knowledge graph is increasingly common. Most studies benchmark the output on innovation metrics; few ask how the form of the graph given to the model affects what it produces, and at what cost. We test this on a microwave oven described as a graph (123 nodes, 246 edges) linking user needs to the functions, parts and technologies that serve them. For each of 13 needs, Claude Haiku 5.5 got the whole graph, only the slice relevant to that need, a random slice of the same size, or no graph, at four levels of thinking effort. Then Claude Sonnet 5.5 scored the resulting 2,892 ideas from 580 runs blind, and we counted top ideas (the best 12%) per 100k tokens.
The relevant slice of the graph worked best: 8.8 top ideas per 100k tokens, about three times the full graph, and more efficient on 10 of 13 needs. Per token, the full graph did worse than no graph at all (about 3 against 6.4): its idea sets were judged better, but its extra context cost more than it found. A random slice also trailed ''no graph'' condition, so relevance, not quantity, is what helps. Thinking longer raised quality but not efficiency, and changing the model's persona did not help. An expert who read every idea found that even the best mostly restate existing technology: the model retrieves the state of the art more than it invents.


\end{abstract}
