\begin{abstract}
Research on automating innovation with AI mostly compares outcomes: LLM ideas against human ones, people with AI against people without. Such benchmarks expire as models improve and get cheaper. We ask a more durable question: how should the problem be presented to the model, measured as outcome per token? A hand-built innovation matrix of a microwave oven (123 nodes, 246 typed edges) was given to one model in four forms: the full graph, the vertical slice for the target market need, a random slice of the same size, or no graph. The model applied the SUBSTITUTE innovation operator to 13 market needs at four levels of thinking effort (580 calls, 2,892 ideas), and a second model judged the ideas blind. Because brainstorming is a search for the rare excellent idea, the main measure is yield: top ideas (the top 12\% of all ideas) per 100k tokens. At high effort, where all four forms were run, yields rank no graph (6.4) $\approx$ market-need slice (5.9) $>$ random slice (4.1) $>$ full graph (3.1): the full graph writes the best-rated idea sets but no more top ideas, and per token it is the least efficient form, while a random slice of the same size falls well behind the market-need slice, so relevance, not size, drives the yield. The overall best is the market-need slice of the graph at medium effort, 8.8 top ideas per 100k tokens spent, about three times more than the full graph at any effort and more efficient than it on 10 of 13 market needs. More thinking effort raises quality but not yield, and in an exploratory follow-up no persona beat a plain innovation engineer. An expert reading of all ideas finds that even the top-rated ones restate the state of the art: the model behaves as a retrieval system over a lossy copy of its corpus, and like any retrieval system it is most efficient when the query is narrow.
\end{abstract}
