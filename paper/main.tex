\begin{abstract}

In AI-assisted product ideation, describing the problem as a knowledge graph is an attractive way to represent the solution space, and one that seems well suited to LLM-powered combinatorial innovation and search. Yet most studies benchmark the output on innovation metrics; few ask how the form of the graph given to the model affects what it produces, and at what cost. We test this on a microwave oven described as a graph (123 nodes, 246 edges) linking user needs to the functions, parts and technologies that serve them. For each of 13 needs, \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} got either (i) the whole graph, (ii) only the slice relevant to that need, (iii) a random slice of the same size, or (iv) no graph, at four levels of thinking effort. \texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5} then scored the resulting 2,892 ideas from 580 runs blind, and we counted top ideas (roughly the best 12\%) per 100k tokens. A follow-up asked three models (\texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}, \texttt{claude-opus-5-5} and \texttt{claude-fable-5-1}) for 50 ideas per call on one fixed market need, at a fixed (medium) effort, adding 6,091 more ideas.

Attaching the full graph to the prompt never paid off. In the first study it yielded about 3 top ideas per 100k tokens, against 8.8 for the relevant slice and 6.4 for no graph. In the follow-up it was the least efficient condition for every model, at about half the yield of the slice or no graph for Haiku and Fable. For the stronger models the full graph did not lower the share of good ideas; it only added cost. Among the ways of giving the graph, the relevant slice worked best, but within the follow-up's uncertainty it was no better than giving no graph at all. The stronger models found two to three times as many top ideas per token, yet at list prices Haiku found 20 to 40 times more per dollar, an advantage that holds when an automated judge screens the ideas but reverses when people read them. Thinking longer raised quality but not efficiency. An expert who read every idea of the first study found that even the best mostly restate existing technology: the model retrieves the state of the art more than it invents.

\end{abstract}
