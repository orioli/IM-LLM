\begin{abstract}

In AI-assisted product ideation, describing the problem as a knowledge graph is a priori a rational and atractive way to represent the solution space that seems adept to facilitate LMM-powered combinatorial innovation or search. However, most studies benchmark the output on innovation metrics; few ask how the form of the graph given to the model affects what it produces, and at what cost. We test this on a microwave oven described as a graph (123 nodes, 246 edges) linking user needs to the functions, parts and technologies that serve them. For each of 13 needs, \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} got either (i) the whole graph, (ii) only the slice relevant to that need, (iii) a random slice of the same size, or (iv) no graph. Four levels of thinking effort were tested. Then \texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5} scored the resulting 2,892 ideas from 580 runs blind, and we counted top ideas (the best 12\%) per 100k tokens. A follow-up test asked three models (\texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}, \texttt{claude-opus-5-5}, \texttt{claude-fable-5-1}) for 50 ideas per call on one fixed need, adding about 6,000 more ideas.

Attaching the full graph to the prompt never paid off. In the first study it yielded about 3 top ideas per 100k tokens, against 8.8 for the relevant slice and 6.4 for no graph. In the follow-up it was the least efficient condition for every model, with roughly half the yield of the alternatives. Using  stronger models did not lower the share of good ideas; it only added significant cost. Among the ways of giving the graph, the relevant slice worked best, but it was statistically no better than ''no graph''. Stronger models found up to twice as many top ideas per token. Per dollar spent Haiku found 20 to 40 times more ideas per dollar. Thinking longer raised quality but not efficiency. An expert who read every idea found that even the best mostly restate existing common knowledge: the model retrieves the state of the art more than it invents.


\end{abstract}
