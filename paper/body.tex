\hypertarget{introduction}{%
\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}}

Most research on AI for innovation asks about outcomes: whether LLM-generated ideas rival human ones \citep{Si2024, Terwiesch2026}, whether AI assistance elevates individual or team creativity \citep{Doshi2024, Anderson2024}, and whether agentic pipelines outperform single prompts or human designers \citep{Liu2026, Massoudi2026}. Yet outcome benchmarks against human baselines expire rapidly as model capabilities expand and token prices fall. A more enduring question is how to \textbf{present the problem} to the model: which representation of a product allows generative AI to turn compute into standout innovation concepts most efficiently.

In product ideation, engineering design, and service innovation, problem representations range from business model canvases and user journey maps to design structure matrices and typed knowledge graphs \citep{ed2024, Steward1981, Gero1990}. Here, we focus on the \emph{innovation matrix} (IM) of Leo Tchirsky \citep{berengueres2014}---a layered, typed graph connecting market needs to functions, components, enabling technologies, and scientific fields. While cost-aware evaluation is now standard for LLM reasoning and agent benchmarks \citep{Chen2024d, Wang2024, Kapoor2025}, generative ideation is still largely judged by average scores without accounting for token expenditure or the extreme-value nature of brainstorming \citep{Girotra2010, Dahan2001}.

To address this gap, we examine how problem representations and reasoning allocations govern AI brainstorming efficiency across four questions: which graph representation yields the most top ideas per token (RQ1); whether structural relevance or raw prompt length drives performance (RQ2); how investing tokens in context compares with spending them on thinking effort (RQ3); and whether the effect of the representation holds across models, and what each model costs per top idea (RQ4). We test these questions on a countertop microwave oven represented as an innovation matrix with 123 nodes and 246 edges, using the \emph{Substitute} operator from the SCAMPER ideation framework \citep{Eberle1971, Osborn1953}. We compare four prompt representations: the full graph (A), a targeted vertical slice of the active market need (functions, components, technologies, fields, linked constraints and actors; B), a size-matched random slice (R), and a no-graph control (C). In Experiment 1 (RQ1--RQ3), \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} receives each representation for 13 market needs at four thinking-effort levels, in 580 API calls generating 2,892 ideas. In Experiment 2 (RQ4), three models, \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5}, \texttt{claude-opus-5-5} and \texttt{claude-fable-5-1}, each receive the four representations for one market need and are asked for 50 ideas per call, in 120 calls generating 6,091 ideas. All ideas are scored blind by \texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5}, and Experiment 1's ideas were also read by an expert. We measure \emph{yield}: top ideas (score $\ge 4.0/5.0$; the top $\sim$12\% in Experiment 1) per 100k tokens spent, and in Experiment 2 also per US dollar at list price.

Our primary finding is that prompting with a targeted market-need slice at medium effort achieves peak yield (8.8 top ideas per 100k tokens), nearly tripling the efficiency of the full graph. Note here that the size of the slice is about one third that of the full graph. Although the full graph produces more specific average sets, it yields no more top-tail ideas while doubling prompt costs. Furthermore, scaling thinking effort raises average linguistic quality but fails to improve token efficiency. Together, these findings make three contributions: they reframe AI ideation around representation efficiency (outcome per token) rather than ephemeral human baselines; they establish yield as a non-saturating tail metric that captures extreme-value creative discovery where mean scores mislead; and they provide an open, controlled empirical comparison of product-graph representations and thinking effort, with all prompts, calls, and judgments released.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{related-work} reviews related work on design representations, LLM ideation workflows, and cost-aware evaluation. Section~\ref{methods} details the product graph, prompt representations, experimental design, and scoring metrics. Section~\ref{results} presents the findings for RQ1--RQ3 (Experiment 1) and RQ4 (Experiment 2), and robustness checks. Finally, Section~\ref{discussion-and-conclusion} discusses implications for design thinking and outlines directions for graph-guided AI innovation.

\section{Related work}
\label{related-work}

\subsection{Innovation Matrix background}

Structured descriptions that link what customers want to what a product does and what it is made of are older than computing in design. Quality function deployment began in Japanese manufacturing in the late 1960s, was taken up by Toyota and its suppliers in the 1970s, and reached Western industry as the ``house of quality'' \citep{ed2024,Hauser1988,Chan2002}. Its matrices relate customer requirements to engineering characteristics, and then to parts and processes, so that every design decision can be traced to a market need. Related schemes followed, each making a different link explicit: morphological analysis lays out the alternatives for each function \citep{Zwicky1967,Ritchey2006,Ritchey2018}; the design structure matrix records the dependencies between components \citep{Steward1981,Browning2001,Eppinger2012}; function--behaviour--structure separates what a design is for from what it is \citep{Gero1990,Gero2004}; the functional basis standardises the vocabulary of functions and flows \citep{Stone2000,Hirtz2002}; and technology roadmaps link market needs to products and technologies over time \citep{Phaal2004}. The innovation matrix used here \citep{berengueres2014} combines several of these links in one layered graph, from market needs through functions and components to technologies and scientific fields (it is distinct from the design-led innovation matrix of \citet{Kyffin2009}). All of these were made for people, typically a team in front of a wall chart, who read them selectively.

\subsection{LLMs that innovate with design methods}

Language models were first used as concept generators \citep{Zhu2022,Ma2023} and compared with people: their concepts are less diverse than crowdsourced ones \citep{Ma2025}, their research ideas are judged more novel but slightly less feasible than experts' \citep{Si2024}, and the effects of working with them are mixed: stories became more creative but more similar to each other \citep{Doshi2024}, and groups less semantically diverse \citep{Anderson2024}, whereas high exposure raised collective idea diversity without changing the creativity of individual ideas \citep{Ashkinaze2025}, and an image generator led to more design fixation and fewer, less varied and less original ideas \citep{Wadinambiarachchi2024}. Large language models are often conceptualized as a lossy, blurry copy of the web \citep{Chiang2023}, distilling vast online corpora into compact neural weights \citep{Karpathy2023}. Recent systems give the model the structured methods above. An FBS ontology steers concept generation \citep{Chen2024a}; generated text and images enrich concepts \citep{Chen2024}; TRIZ is applied by single models and by agent teams \citep{Chen2024c,Jiang2025,Szczepanik2025}; morphological analysis is automated \citep{Chen2024b}; and agentic workflows chain whole design processes \citep{Ding2023,Massoudi2026}. iDesignGPT \citep{Liu2026} goes furthest: agent clusters run problem definition with QFD and Kano analysis, then functional decomposition, morphological analysis, SCAMPER \citep{Eberle1971} and TRIZ, and produce a product design specification.

\subsection{What ``innovative'' is checked against}\label{what-innovative-is-checked-against}

These systems claim to innovate, but the claim usually rests on ratings: experts or a model score novelty and originality, or embedding distances measure how far the generated concepts spread from each other. iDesignGPT, for example, reports ``competitive novelty and consistently higher originality and modularity'' than GPT-4o zero-shot, GPT-4o chain-of-thought and Deepseek-r1, on the basis of expert scores and semantic novelty metrics; patents are searched as an input to the workflow, and originality is scored as the embedding similarity of the generated designs to patents (lower is more original), but the score ranks designs against each other and does not establish whether an idea already exists \citep{Liu2026}. This is typical. Novelty is judged relative to other generated ideas or to a rater's memory, seldom against what already exists, although patent-based measures of technological novelty \citep{Fleming2001,Verhoeven2016} and citation-based measures of novel combinations in the literature \citep{Uzzi2013} are well established, and patent-derived knowledge networks are available for design \citep{Sarica2020,Luo2021}. An idea a rater finds novel may be on the market already. Our own study shares this limitation: its judge rates novelty from knowledge, without a prior-art search, and several of its top-rated ideas, such as solid-state RF heating and RF thawing, already exist in industry, as the judge itself noted. A second gap is cost: few of these systems report what their outputs cost, and none compares configurations at equal cost, so a more elaborate pipeline cannot be weighed against a simpler one. To our knowledge, none of these studies reports a patent resulting from its generated concepts. Nor do the workflows described include collecting first-hand information in the field (\emph{genchi genbutsu}, ``go and see'' \citep{berengueres2014}).

\subsection{Context and cost in LLMs}

More context is not free even when it fits in the window. In practice, models exhibit an ``effective context window'' far smaller than their advertised capacity, with performance degrading sharply as input token length increases \citep{Paulsen2026}, a limitation attributed to the left-skewed frequency of relative positions in training, which leaves long-distance positions under-trained \citep{An2024}. Models use information in the middle of long inputs poorly \citep{Liu2024}, their reasoning degrades as inputs grow long well before the limit \citep{Levy2024}, and they struggle when relevant content must be inferred rather than matched \citep{Modarressi2025}; retrieval is much cheaper than long context, which still performs better when resourced sufficiently, and routing between the two reaches comparable performance at lower cost \citep{Li2024}. Elsewhere, cost is becoming a first-class outcome: cascades and routers match strong models at lower cost \citep{Chen2024d,Ong2024,Ding2024}, reasoning strategies are compared at equal token budgets \citep{Wang2024,Han2025}, and agent benchmarks report accuracy together with cost \citep{Kapoor2025,Wang2025}. This  concerns tasks with defined right answers. Ideation, as mentioned, has no defined right answers. Its efficiency is  rarely considered.

\subsection{Measuring ideation}

For lack of objective measures (beyond patentability), currently ideation with AI is using linguistic-like tools. It is assessed by quantity, quality, (linguistic) novelty and (linguistic) variety \citep{Shah2003}, or by novelty and usefulness together \citep{Sarkar2011}. But a brainstorm is worth its best ideas, since the rest are discarded: idea-generation processes should be compared on the quality of the best idea, not the average \citep{Girotra2010}, and extreme-value models describe how the best of many concepts improves with the number tested \citep{Dahan2001}. Mean scores, the usual summary in LLM ideation studies, hide exactly this difference. To avoid this trap, here we combine the tail and the cost in one measure, top ideas per token, and vary only how the product is presented, holding the model, the method and the prompt fixed.

\hypertarget{product-graph}{%
\subsection{Product graph}\label{product-graph}}

\textbf{The innovation matrix.} The innovation matrix (IM) of Leo Tchirsky, as taught in design-thinking workshops \citep{berengueres2014}, describes a product as stacked layers: the \textbf{market needs} it serves, the \textbf{functions} that serve them, the \textbf{components} that perform the functions, the \textbf{technologies} that make the components possible, and the scientific \textbf{fields} behind those technologies (Figure \ref{fig:im_brownbook}). Reading upwards answers \emph{why} a part exists; reading downwards answers \emph{how} a market need is met. Innovation happens by changing a link: a new technology enables a different component, a component takes over another's function, or a function is found to serve a market need nobody had linked it to. In a workshop the matrix is drawn on a wall; here it is a typed graph that a model can read.

\textbf{Edges} carry one of twelve types. Those that build the layer stack are \texttt{has\_component}, \texttt{performs\_function}, \texttt{enables\_function}, \texttt{addresses\_need}, \texttt{enabled\_by} and \texttt{rooted\_in}. Component-level flows are \texttt{feeds} and \texttt{actuates}. Constraint links are \texttt{causes}, \texttt{contradicts} and \texttt{partially\_mitigates}. Weak associations are \texttt{relevant\_to}. A thirteenth type, \texttt{resolves}, is declared in the schema for agents to use when a new node replaces or solves an existing one; it does not occur in the base graph.

Each node has an identifier, type, label and a one-line detail. The file begins with a meta block that names the layers and defines the edge types.

\hypertarget{task-and-prompt}{%
\subsection{Task and prompt}\label{task-and-prompt}}

All calls use one prompt template (\texttt{template\_prompt.txt}, Appendix~\ref{generation-prompt}). It asks the model to innovate the product ``based on the graph given'', encourages radical and wild ideas, and asks it to favour innovations that serve the target market need. Placeholders are filled by literal substitution: product name, target market need identifier and label, operator name and definition, the graph file, and the number of ideas.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Operator.} The \emph{Substitute} operator from SCAMPER \citep{Eberle1971} in every call: ``Identify components and the functions they perform, then replace them with something cheaper or simpler that fulfils the same function equally well or better.''
\item
  \textbf{Worked example.} One example of an innovation the brief likes, taken from the authors' workshop material \citep{berengueres2014}. The door's mesh and the interior lamp both serve the user's need to see the food, so they can be replaced together by a webcam, or by a transparent casing that needs no lamp.
\item
  \textbf{Output.} Exactly 5 ideas as JSON. Each idea has a title; the identifiers of existing nodes it was applied to (\emph{anchors}); new nodes (type, label, one-line detail); new edges; and a one-sentence rationale for how it serves the target market need.
\end{itemize}

The prompt is identical across conditions except for the graph file. In condition C, the ``graph given'' is only the schema and the target market need.

\item
  \textbf{Logging.} The runner appends one JSON line per call to an append-only log (\texttt{runs/rq1\_full.jsonl}). Each line holds the run identifier (condition, market need, effort, replicate), the graph file's identifier and hash, the prompt hash, model, output limit, input and output tokens, latency, stop reason, the parsed ideas and the raw response. Runs are resumable, and cells can be added later without rerunning earlier ones.
\end{itemize}

The brief shown to the judge gave the product, target market need, operator, and the instruction that radical, wild ideas serving the market need were wanted.

\hypertarget{measures}{%
\subsection{Measures}\label{measures}}

\textbf{Cost.} Input, output and total tokens per call, from the API's usage report.

\textbf{Use of the graph} (computed without model calls, from the parsed ideas):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{anchors outside the market-need slice}: the share of anchors that are existing nodes outside B's slice for that market need;
\item
  \emph{anchors not in the graph}: the share of anchors that match no node identifier in the base graph;
\item
  \emph{ideas echoing the worked example}: ideas whose title or new nodes mention a webcam, camera or transparency;
\item
  \emph{new nodes per idea}, and \emph{words per idea} in the text the judge sees.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Top tail.} A \textbf{top idea} is an idea whose individual score is at least 4.0, that is, one the judge rates 4 or higher on average across relevance, novelty, feasibility and specificity. The threshold is a fixed point on the judge's scale, not a percentile, so the share of ideas that clear it depends on what is being judged. Scores move in steps of 0.25 (one step of a 1--5 rating, averaged over four criteria), so no exact percentile cut exists; in Experiment 1, 4.0 is the step closest to the top 10\% and keeps 12.3\% of all ideas (355 of 2,892). As a stricter check, 4.25 keeps the top 2.4\% (68 ideas).

\textbf{What ``top'' means, and what it does not.} A top idea is top \emph{relative to this setup}: one judge model, one rubric, one brief, one product and one operator. It is not a global benchmark of idea quality or of innovativeness. A score of 4.0 says the judge found the idea relevant, plausible and concrete for this brief; it does not say the idea is new to the world (Section~\ref{sample of genrated ideas} shows that most top ideas restate existing technology), and it cannot be compared with scores from other judges, rubrics or studies. Within the study, the fixed threshold makes arms comparable: an arm that produces more ideas the judge rates highly gets more top ideas. A percentile cut per arm or per model would instead give every arm the same share by construction and hide exactly the differences being measured.

Target market needs differ widely in how easily they yield top ideas (from 3\% to 29\% of ideas at 4.0), and the xhigh arms cover only 6 market needs, so every rate is computed per market need and then averaged over the market needs an arm was run on, each market need weighing the same. For each arm:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{top-idea rate}: the market-need-averaged share of the arm's ideas that are top ideas;
\item
  \textbf{calls with a top idea}: the market-need-averaged share of calls producing at least one top idea;
\item
  \textbf{tokens per top idea}: the arm's total tokens divided by its number of top ideas;
\item
  \textbf{chance of a top idea for a budget} \(b\): the probability of at least one top idea when \(b\) tokens are spent on whole calls of that arm, averaged over market needs. For need \(j\), with \(p_j\) the share of calls containing a top idea and \(\bar t_j\) the mean tokens per call, \(k = \lfloor b / \bar t_j \rfloor\) calls succeed with probability \(1 - (1 - p_j)^k\). Calls are treated as independent. Because 5 calls per market need often give a raw share of exactly 0 or 1, \(p_j\) is the Jeffreys estimate \((h_j + \tfrac12)/(n_j + 1)\), where \(h_j\) of \(n_j\) calls contained a top idea.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Mean idea score.} The market-need-averaged mean individual score per arm, and the mean of each criterion.

\hypertarget{statistics}{%
\subsection{Statistics}\label{statistics}}

\textbf{Pairwise judging.} The unit is the pair, not the verdict: the two presentation orders of a pair are not independent, so they are combined first. For each comparison:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the \emph{win rate} of the first-named arm, with ties counting as half a win;
\item
  a Wilson 95\% confidence interval;
\item
  an exact two-sided sign test on wins against losses, ties excluded;
\item
  the \emph{order agreement}, the share of pairs that received the same verdict in both orders;
\item
  the mean score difference per criterion.
\end{itemize}

Whether the market-need slice is no worse than the full graph is tested as non-inferiority with a margin of 45\%: B is non-inferior if the lower bound of its win-rate interval against A is above 45\%.

To check whether judges favour longer sets, each pairwise record stores the number of words each side showed the judge. The share of decided pairs won by the longer set is reported next to each win rate.

\textbf{Top tail.} 95\% confidence intervals for the top-idea rate and the call rate come from 2,000 bootstrap resamples. Each resample redraws calls with replacement within each market need, which keeps the five ideas of a call together and the market-need-averaged weighting fixed.

No correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Per-market-need results rest on 5 calls or pairs each and are reported only as patterns.

\textbf{Prompt.} The prompt is Experiment 1's word for word, except for the one line that sets the number of ideas. Finally, holding effort fixed leaves representation and model as the only factors that vary. \textbf{Judging.} Every idea was scored individually by the judge of Experiment 1, with the same prompt and the same four criteria. \textbf{Measures.}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Top idea:} an idea scoring at least 4.0, the same fixed threshold as in Experiment 1. Because the threshold is a point on the scale and not a percentile, the share of ideas clearing it differs by model: 6.1\% of Haiku's ideas (126 of 2,080), 13.6\% of Opus's (270 of 1,985) and 14.7\% of Fable's (295 of 2,001), against 12.3\% in Experiment 1. \item

Thinking tokens are billed as output. No batch or caching discount is applied. The yield per dollar is an arm's top ideas divided by its cost. The judge's cost is the same for every model and is not included.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Statistics.} With one market need, the bootstrap resamples the 10 calls of each arm with replacement (2,000 resamples), keeping each call's ideas and tokens together. Arms are compared by these intervals. The distribution of single-call yields is shown alongside as a description of how much individual calls vary, not as a test. No correction for multiple comparisons is applied.

\textbf{Plan and exploration.} The Haiku run was planned and its hypotheses recorded on 2026-10-19, before any 50-idea call. Those hypotheses concern how many distinct ideas each representation reaches and whether later ideas stay on the need; they require coding every idea into a codebook of moves, which is in progress and not used here. The Opus and Fable runs were added on 2026-10-20, after the Haiku scores had been seen, and the yield per dollar after that. All RQ4 results are therefore exploratory.

\textbf{Limitations specific to Experiment 2.}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Scores are not comparable with Experiment 1.} Asked for 50 ideas, the models write shorter ideas (about 35 words per idea for all three models, against about 100 in Experiment 1), which the judge scores lower, so top-idea rates and yields are compared within Experiment 2 only.
\item
  \emph{The judge is weaker than two of the generators.} Sonnet judges ideas from Opus and Fable, and may not recognise the best ideas of a stronger model. Judge and generators also come from one model family.
\item
  \emph{Scope.} One product, one market need, one effort level and 10 calls per arm.
\item
  \emph{Prices change.} List prices are a snapshot. The ranking per dollar would change with discounts or new prices, while the yield per token would not.
\end{itemize}




\hypertarget{sample of genrated ideas}{%
\subsection{Sample of generated ideas}\label{sample of genrated ideas}}

One of the authors read all the ideas from Experiment 1, which led to Experiment 2. LLM-ideation studies usually assess novelty with embedding measures such as the semantic distance between ideas, which show how the ideas differ from each other but not from prior art. Read against what is already known in the domain, three things stood out:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{The top tier is state of the art, not invention.} Two ideas from the judge's top decile from Experiment 1:

  \begin{quote}
  \texttt{A\_M13\_high\_r03} --- \emph{Solid-State RF Core: Magnetron and HV Stack Replaced by GaN Amplifiers}
  
  \texttt{B\_M13\_low\_r01} --- \emph{Magnetron-free solid-state RF cooking head}
  \end{quote}

  As engineering these are sound; as innovation their value is close to zero. Gallium-nitride power semiconductors made it practical, in the early 2020s, to retire the noisy magnetron and convert the whole RF subsystem to solid state, with gains in weight, noise and vibration, and in the precision of power delivery and control. Dozens of patents apply GaN to microwave ovens and the first commercial products exist; the open problem is cost, at present roughly ten times the magnetron subsystem. A prior-art check would score these ideas at the floor, where the judge's novelty criterion put them near the ceiling.

  The middle of the ranking tells the same story. Five ideas drawn from around the median: 
  
\begin{quote}

\emph{Sliding RF-Choke Curtain Replaces the Hinged Door and Push Button}; 

\emph{Photoluminescent cavity glow replaces the 20--25 W lamp}; 

\emph{Sputter-Listening Auto-Stop replaces the timer}; 

\emph{Acoustic Thaw: Ultrasonic Pre-Defrost Replacing Low-Power Cycling}; 

\emph{Phased-Array Field Sweep (No Turntable)}. 
  
\end{quote}
  
  Every one of them is prior art or commercially available: sensor-based auto-stop has been standard in consumer ovens for decades, solid-state ovens already steer the field electronically instead of rotating the food, and ultrasound-assisted thawing is established in food processing. From the top of the ranking to its middle, the judge orders the state of the art; it does not separate the new from the known.
\item
  \textbf{Scores are biased towards fit.} A sample from the bottom decile: 

    \begin{quote}

  \emph{Ghost Lamp Vision: Replace the Interior Lamp With Microwave-Transparent Walls}; 

  \emph{GaN Universal-Mains Power Stage Replaces HV Transformer Stack}; 
  
  \emph{Capacitor-Free 48 V Bus Power Stage}; 
  
  \emph{Direct-Coupled Magnetron Pod (No Waveguide Duct)}. 
  
  \end{quote}
  
  The GaN power stage here is not materially different from the GaN ideas at the top of the ranking. Part of the gap is defensible, since the judge marks a power-supply substitution down on relevance when it is aimed at a market need it cannot serve; but the same technical move appearing at both ends of the ranking shows that an AI ranker is not entirely consistent, and that its scores reflect the fit between a move and the target market need as much as the move itself.
\item
  \textbf{Diversity.} A topic analysis (Nvivo; not shown) finds the same ideas concentrated in a few recurring topical pathways: solid-state GaN RF generation, transparent or lamp-free enclosures, simplifications of the RF chain, and sensor-based doneness feedback. This should not surprise anyone: each call asked for five ideas under one operator, and further calls revisit the same pathways more often than they open new ones.
\end{enumerate}

Karpathy has described a language model as ``a lossy compression of the internet'' \citep{Karpathy2023}, and the compressed corpus includes the patent and product literature of this domain. Read this way, the generator behaved as a retrieval system: excellent at surfacing the engineering moves that bear on a market need, which is also why the \ensuremath{\sim}11,000-token full-graph prompt and the \ensuremath{\sim}1,000-token no-graph prompt converge on the same few pathways. This matches the argument that language models excel at induction and increasingly at deduction, but not at abduction, the leap to new premises that genuine invention requires \citep{Zahavy2026}. The setup cannot be blamed for presenting the state of the art as innovation, if innovation is defined as what no one has patented before. What the experiment measures is therefore the efficiency with which a representation surfaces strong, relevant engineering moves, the raw material of ideation, not patentable novelty; the same reading applies to published systems whose ``innovative'' output is rated rather than checked against prior art (Section~\ref{what-innovative-is-checked-against}).

The compression is of what has been written, and what is written about microwave ovens is mostly technology. Reading all the ideas, what is absent is as telling as what is present. Cost appears nowhere as the object of innovation, although the graph carries an explicit low-cost constraint (X12) and cost is the main force selecting microwave innovations: the magnetron has outlived every technically superior alternative because fifty years of manufacturing scale have made it extremely cheap, and that advantage is self-reinforcing, a path dependency that any real substitution must pay its way out of. Nothing in the ideas reflects the open-source hardware movement and its community-designed and community-repaired appliances, and nothing uses the fact that most households run a microwave next to a conventional oven, so that several market needs, crisping and browning among them, are already served by the appliance beside it. This knowledge lives in markets, communities and kitchens more than in patents and specification sheets, so the lossy copy retrieves the recorded state of the art and misses the selection environment that decides which of those ideas becomes an innovation.

\hypertarget{results}{%
\section{Results}\label{results}}

\hypertarget{summary}{%
\subsection{Summary}\label{summary}}

The aim of brainstorming is the rare great idea, not a good average, so the main measure here is the \textbf{top tail}: how often an arm produces a top idea (score $\ge 4.0$, the top $\sim$12\% of Experiment 1's ideas), and what each such idea costs.

\textbf{RQ1. Which representation yields the most top ideas per token?} (Section~\ref{rq1})

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A relevant slice of the graph finds top ideas fastest per token.} Counted against tokens spent, top ideas accumulate in a straight line for every arm, so each arm has a yield: top ideas per 100k tokens. The market-need slice (B) at medium effort yields 8.8, about three times the full graph (A) at any effort (2.6--3.2). B's top-idea rate is also the highest at equal effort (15\% at medium and high, against 10\% for no graph and for a random slice of the same size). In Experiment 2 (RQ4), where every representation ran at the same effort, the slice matched but did not beat no graph, for all three models.
\item
  \textbf{The efficiency gain holds market need by market need, and is largest for small slices.} The slice is more efficient than the full graph on 10 of 13 market needs (sign test p = 0.039), and no graph beats a random slice on 10 of 12. The slice's lead is 3--5$\times$ for market needs whose slice has 15--28 nodes, and 1--2$\times$ for those with 45--51 nodes (exploratory).
\item
  \textbf{No graph is surprisingly efficient.} Its calls are the cheapest, so despite a low top-idea rate its yield (6.4) beats the random slice (4.1) and the full graph (3.1), and is close to the slice at high effort (5.9).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{RQ2. Does the amount or the relevance of the graph matter?} (Section~\ref{rq2})

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{The full graph (A) writes the best idea \emph{sets} but does not find more top ideas.} Judged as whole sets, A beats B 64\% of the time and no graph 74\% of the time. But its top-idea rate is no higher than B's, and its yield is about 3 top ideas per 100k tokens at every effort, half or less of B's: its advantage is the specificity of an average set, not more standout ideas.
\item
  \textbf{Relevance vs size is only partly settled.} In the top tail, the market-need slice beats the size-matched random slice (top-idea rate 14.7\% vs 9.8\%; yield 5.9 vs 4.1 per 100k tokens; the slice's slope is higher in 99\% of paired bootstrap draws). Judged as whole sets, the two cannot be told apart (B over R 54\%, p = 0.55). What a relevant slice adds shows up in the best ideas, not in the average set.
\item
  \textbf{A graph helps, clearly so when it is the full graph.} A beats C on whole sets (74\%, p \textless{} 0.001); B beats C less clearly (62\%, p = 0.053) but has a higher top-idea rate (14.7\% vs 10.2\%).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{RQ3. How does spending tokens on context compare with spending them on thinking effort?} (Section~\ref{rq3})


\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Thinking effort is the strongest lever on quality, but not on yield.} High beats low in about 93\% of set comparisons, and xhigh produces the highest top-idea rates of all (19--21\%). But each call costs proportionally more, so yield does not rise with effort: for the full graph it stays at about 3 per 100k tokens from low to xhigh, and for the slice it peaks at medium (8.8) and falls at high (5.9) and xhigh (3.1).
\end{itemize}


\textbf{RQ4. Does the effect of the representation hold across models, and what does each model cost per top idea?} (Section~\ref{rq4})

Asked for 50 ideas per call on market need M6, all three models found top ideas least efficiently with the full graph: per token, its yield was about half that of the slice or no graph for Haiku (9.4 against 20.4 and 22.2 top ideas per 100k tokens) and Fable (26.4 against 51.3 and 53.6), and the lowest for Opus (26.3 against 39.9 and 34.8). The slice and no graph were indistinguishable for every model. For Opus and Fable the full graph did not lower the share of top ideas; it only cost more. The stronger models found two to three times as many top ideas per token as Haiku, but at list prices Haiku found about 20 times more per dollar than Opus and about 40 times more than Fable. Exploratory.

\hypertarget{rq2}{%
\subsection{RQ2: Does the amount or the relevance of the graph matter?}\label{rq2}}

\textbf{Answer.} Relevance, not amount. More graph buys better average sets: the full graph wins 64--65\% of set comparisons against the market-need slice, mainly on specificity, but its top-idea rate is no higher, so at about twice the tokens per call it finds fewer top ideas per token. Relevant structure helps in the top tail: the market-need slice out-yields a random slice of the same size in 99\% of paired bootstrap draws, although whole-set judging cannot tell the two apart. For Haiku, irrelevant structure is worse than none: no graph is more efficient than the random slice on 10 of 12 market needs. Stronger models are less affected: in Experiment 2 the random slice was about as efficient as no graph for Opus, and between the full graph and no graph for Fable (Section~\ref{rq4}).

\hypertarget{relevance-top-tail}{%
\subsubsection{Relevance in the top tail: market-need slice, random slice and no graph}\label{relevance-top-tail}}
The market-need slice (B) and the random slice (R) have the same number of nodes per layer, so comparing them separates relevance from amount; no graph (C) is the floor.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Top-idea rate, B vs R:} at the same effort and nearly the same size, the market-need slice yields top ideas at 14.7\% against 9.8\% for the random slice; the confidence intervals just touch. The whole-set judging does not confirm this (Section~\ref{pairwise-set-judging-the-controls-high-effort}).
\item
  \textbf{Yield, B vs R:} at high effort, B yields 5.9 and R 4.1; the 95\% intervals just touch (4.9--7.0 against 3.1--5.2), but pairing the two arms' bootstrap draws, B's slope is the higher in 99\% of them (Figure \ref{fig:spread}), equivalent to a one-sided bootstrap $p \approx 0.01$. R costs about as much per call as B, so the difference is in what the calls find.
\item
  \textbf{Per market need, B vs R is not robust} (9 of 13, p = 0.27), in line with the pooled yield (intervals touching) and the set judging (no difference).
\item
  \textbf{Graph vs none:} a relevant slice yields more top ideas than no graph (14.7\% vs 10.2\%); a random slice does not (9.8\%). The full graph is in between (13.5\%).
\item
  \textbf{No graph is more efficient than a random slice on 10 of 12 decided market needs (p = 0.039).} Showing the model structure that is not about the problem costs more per top idea than showing it nothing.
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{rq3}{%
\subsection{RQ3: How does spending tokens on context compare with spending them on thinking effort?}\label{rq3}}

\textbf{Answer.} Thinking effort raises quality; the choice of context raises efficiency. High effort beats low in 92--94\% of set comparisons and xhigh gives the highest top-idea rates (19--21\%), but the cost per call rises in step, so yield does not improve: it is flat for the full graph and peaks at medium effort for the slice. \textbf{The full graph is the least efficient representation for every model.} Per token, its yield is about half that of the slice and of no graph for Haiku and Fable, and the lowest of the four for Opus. \textbf{The random slice} was as poor as the full graph for Haiku but close to the slice and no graph for Opus, and in between for Fable. A stronger model seems less hurt by irrelevant context of moderate size.

\subsubsection{Unit economics}\label{rq4-economics}

\textbf{Per token, the stronger models are about two to three times as productive.} With the slice or no graph, Opus found 35--40 and Fable 51--54 top ideas per 100k tokens, against 20--22 for Haiku. All three used similar numbers of tokens per call (Table~\ref{tab:gen-exp2}); the difference is the share of ideas that reach the top: 12--17\% for Opus and Fable against 4--8\% for Haiku.

\textbf{Per dollar, the order reverses.} At list prices Opus costs 40 times and Fable 100 times as much per token as Haiku, so Haiku found about 460 top ideas per dollar with the slice or no graph, against about 20 for Opus and 12 for Fable. A top idea cost about \$0.002 with Haiku, \$0.05 with Opus and \$0.09 with Fable. The whole run of 40 calls cost \$0.35 with Haiku, \$13.32 with Opus and \$30.89 with Fable.

\textbf{Per dollar, the full graph's penalty shrinks.} The graph adds only input tokens, which cost a fifth as much as output tokens. Per dollar, Opus's full graph is within the intervals of the other three representations (19.3 against 18.2--22.9); Fable's remains clearly below the slice and no graph (7.5 against 11.9 and 11.4).

\textbf{The net cost depends on who sifts the ideas.} The dollar yield counts only generation. But the top ideas must still be found among all the others, and Haiku makes the sifting longer: it produced one top idea per 16.5 ideas, against one per 7.4 for Opus and 6.8 for Fable. With a screening cost $s$ per idea, the net cost of a top idea is
\[
\text{net cost per top idea} = \frac{\text{generation cost} + s \times \text{ideas generated}}{\text{top ideas}} .
\]
Table~\ref{tab:netcost} evaluates it for the three models, pooled over the four representations, with 95\% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of calls within representation. Screening by an automated judge, as in this study, cost \$0.0012 per idea (\texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5} at list price), and at that cost Haiku is the cheapest per top idea in every resample. Opus becomes cheaper than Haiku once screening costs more than about \$0.005 per idea (95\% CI \$0.004--0.008), and Fable once it costs more than about \$0.01 (\$0.008--0.015). At the cost of a person reading each idea (about 30 seconds at \$60 an hour, \$0.50 per idea), both larger models cost less than half as much per top idea as Haiku in every resample; between them, Opus and Fable cost about the same at any screening cost (Fable cheaper in 83\% of resamples at \$0.50). The content-only re-rating (Section~\ref{rq4-style}) adds that Haiku's top ideas are also somewhat weaker. The cheapest model is therefore the one that matches the screening: a small model when an automated judge filters the output, a larger one when people do.

\subsubsection{Single calls and list length}\label{rq4-calls}

\textbf{Single calls vary widely} (Figure~\ref{fig:rq4}B). Within one arm, a call's yield ranged up to about fourfold (for example 12 to 67 top ideas per 100k tokens for Fable with the random slice), so a single brainstorming call is an unreliable sample of what a configuration delivers. In Experiment 1, with five ideas per call, about half of all calls produced no top idea at all.

\textbf{Asking for 50 ideas instead of 5.} For Haiku on M6, the 50-idea calls had a per-token yield close to that of the 5-idea calls with the slice (20.4 against 25.3) and higher with the other representations (for example 9.4 against 6.3 with the full graph). The two experiments differ in idea length and therefore in score, so this comparison is indicative only.

\hypertarget{change-from-the-pilot}{%
\subsubsection{Change from the pilot}\label{change-from-the-pilot}}

The pilot (2 market needs $\times$ 3 replicates) found the full graph winning 17 of 18 set comparisons. At full scale (13 market needs, 65 pairs) A's set-level advantage shrinks to about 64\%, and on the tail measure it disappears. The pilot's ``more novel but less feasible'' pattern for A did not replicate (it now describes no graph, C). The effort effect and the B-high vs A-low result held. Contrary to the pilot's expectation, quality kept rising from high to xhigh.

\hypertarget{caveats}{%
\subsubsection{Caveats}\label{caveats}}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Single judge model,} with no human calibration yet; a ``top idea'' is one the judge rates highly on relevance, novelty, feasibility and specificity together, a grounded strong idea rather than necessarily the wildest one.
\item
  \textbf{Coarse scores:} individual scores move in 0.25 steps, so the threshold (4.0, top 12.3\%) is the nearest available cut, and the 4.25 tail has too few ideas to separate arms.
\item
  \textbf{Yield assumes a constant rate per token.} The observed running counts are close to straight lines (Figure \ref{fig:yield}), but they depend on the order in which calls are taken; the slope itself does not.
\item
  \textbf{Budget curves assume independent calls} within a market need and use a small-sample estimate of each market need's success rate; they are a model, not observed sequences.
\item
  \textbf{R and C were run only at high effort;} R vs C was not judged pairwise, so whether a random slice beats no graph on whole sets is unknown.
\item
  \textbf{Relevance vs size rests on the tail measure:} the pairwise judging found no difference between B and R.
\item
  \textbf{R controls for node count, not edges or graph distribution:} $R$ matches $B$ in node count and layer composition, but random selection yields substantially sparser induced graphs (2--52 edges vs.\ 11--85 in $B$) and 17\% fewer input tokens. Furthermore, only a single fixed random draw per market need was evaluated rather than an ensemble over multiple random graph realizations, so variance across different random subgraphs is not averaged out.
\item
  \textbf{Length and effort are intertwined;} the pairwise effort effects may be partly inflated by detail bias.
\item
  \textbf{xhigh covers 6 of 13 market needs,} which are slightly easier than average for top ideas; see the matched-needs results.
\item
  \textbf{One product, one operator, one prompt.} The worked example comes from the M8 branch of the graph, which may favour ideas in that area.
\item
  \textbf{Per-market-need results} rest on 5 calls or pairs each and should be read as patterns; the slice-size effect is exploratory (13 market needs, $p \approx 0.04$--$0.13$).
\end{itemize}

\hypertarget{discussion-and-conclusion}{%
\section{Discussion}\label{discussion-and-conclusion}}



This study asked how much of a product's knowledge graph an LLM needs to see in order to brainstorm well, judged not by the quality of its output alone but by how efficiently it turns tokens, and money, into top ideas. An innovation matrix of a microwave oven (123 nodes, 246 edges) was given to a model in four forms: the full graph (A), the vertical slice for the target market need (B), a random slice of the same size (R), or no graph at all (C). In Experiment 1, \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} applied the SUBSTITUTE operator to each of 13 market needs at four levels of thinking effort, producing 2,892 ideas. In Experiment 2, Haiku, \texttt{claude-opus-5-5} and \texttt{claude-fable-5-1} were each asked for 50 ideas per call on one market need. All ideas were judged blind by \texttt{claude-sonnet-5-5}. Brainstorming is a search for the rare great idea, so the main measure was the \textbf{yield}: top ideas (score $\ge 4.0$) per 100k tokens, and in Experiment 2 also per dollar.

The short answer is that, for finding top ideas, the knowledge graph did not pay for itself. Six conclusions support it.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{The full graph never paid off.} In Experiment 1 it yielded about 3 top ideas per 100k tokens at every effort, against 8.8 for the market-need slice at medium effort and 6.4 for no graph; the slice was more efficient on 10 of 13 market needs (sign test p = 0.039). In Experiment 2 it was the least efficient representation for all three models, at about half the yield per token of the slice or no graph for Haiku and Fable.
\item
  \textbf{A relevant slice removes the penalty but adds no gain.} If a graph is given, the market-need slice is the most efficient form, and its lead over the full graph looks largest where the slice is small (exploratory). It is not clearly better than giving no graph: in Experiment 1 no graph was run at high effort only, and in Experiment 2, with every representation at the same effort, the slice and no graph were indistinguishable for Haiku, Opus and Fable alike.
\item
  \textbf{More context buys better average sets, not more great ideas.} Judged as whole sets, the full graph beats the slice (64\%) and no graph (74\%), mainly by being more specific. But its top-idea rate is no higher than the slice's, and its calls cost about twice as much. For Opus and Fable the full graph did not lower the share of top ideas at all; its whole penalty was the cost of the extra input.
\item
  \textbf{Irrelevant context hurts the small model most.} For Haiku, a random slice of the same size as the market-need slice is less efficient than both the slice and no graph (5.9 and 6.4 against 4.1 at high effort; no graph beats it on 10 of 12 market needs, p = 0.039), although whole-set judging cannot tell the two slices apart. For Opus a random slice cost nothing against no graph, and for Fable less than the full graph did.
\item
  \textbf{Thinking effort raises quality, not efficiency.} High effort beats low in about 93\% of set comparisons, and extra-high effort gives the highest top-idea rates (19--21\%). But each call costs proportionally more, so yield does not rise with effort: the full graph stays at about 3 top ideas per 100k tokens from low to extra-high, and the slice peaks at medium.
\item
  \textbf{Model choice is a question of unit economics.} Per token, Opus and Fable found two to three times as many top ideas as Haiku; per dollar at list prices, Haiku found about 20 times more than Opus and 40 times more than Fable. The best model per token is the worst per dollar (exploratory; Section~\ref{rq4}).
\end{enumerate}

These results are bounded. They concern one product, one operator, one judge model and, in Experiment 2, one market need, and a top idea is one this judge rated highly, not an idea checked against prior art. The expert reading suggests what the judge rewards: Haiku behaves as a retrieval system, holding a lossy copy of its training corpus, patents and product literature included, and surfacing the engineering moves that bear on a stated problem (Section~\ref{sample of genrated ideas}). A retrieval system is most efficient when the query is precise, and a stated market need is already a precise query; a large graph adds cost more than direction. Whether larger models do more than retrieve is open. It has been argued that language models are structurally incapable of the abductive ``jump'' to new premises \citep{Zahavy2026}, and that fixed-weight transformer inference lacks the coupling between error and cost that such a jump requires, regardless of model scale \citep{Balani2026}; Experiment 2 is consistent with that view but cannot test it. A content-only re-rating (Section~\ref{rq4-style}) suggests that their lead over Haiku is one of content, while Fable's edge over Opus may be one of presentation; checking the best ideas against prior art would show whether either goes beyond retrieval.

For practice, the advice is plain: state the market need, give the model at most the slice of the graph that serves it, and choose the model by cost per top idea rather than by its reputation.

\textbf{Future work.} Four extensions follow from the limits of this study. First, the many settings that change cost and yield (representation, model, effort, persona, list length) suggest treating prompt configuration as an optimisation problem: Bayesian optimisation or bandit methods could search this space for the cheapest setting that reaches a target number of top ideas for a given product and need, rather than testing a few settings exhaustively. Second, the judge's ratings should be anchored outside the judge: rewriting all ideas into one neutral style before judging would separate content from presentation, and checking the best ideas against patents and products would show whether any of them goes beyond the state of the art. Third, the move coding of Experiment 2, planned before it was run, will show whether the representation changes how many distinct ideas a model reaches, not only how many it rates highly. Finally, the results rest on one product, one operator and one judge; repeating them across products, operators such as TRIZ, and human raters would show how far they generalise.

\hypertarget{implications-for-design-thinking}{%
\subsection{Implications for design thinking}\label{implications-for-design-thinking}}
For design practice, the results point the same way as a familiar design move: framing beats volume. Stating the market need precisely did most of the work. Giving the model the relevant slice of the product graph was the machine version of narrowing the problem, but it did no better than the stated need alone, and the full graph only added cost. Three highlights:

The measure introduced here, \emph{yield} (top ideas per token or per dollar), turns ideation into a budgetable activity: the cost of a given number of strong candidates can be estimated before a session is run, which may also help to design and tune agentic ideation workflows.

None of this makes the product graph useless. It remains a strong tool for systematic search, for example a mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive (MECE) decomposition of the design space, which could help make the combinatorics of innovation explicit rather than leaving them to the model's recall.

Finally, we propose, and leave for future work, the idea of a \emph{diffable} product graph: one that can be compared with another graph to show exactly what differs. Diffing a product's graph against a competitor's, against an earlier version, or against the graph of the needs it should serve would point to where innovation is missing or possible, giving agents a machine friendly operator to find explore the design space.
